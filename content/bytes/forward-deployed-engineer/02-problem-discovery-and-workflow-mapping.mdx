## Why the first request is rarely the requirement

A stakeholder may ask, “Build a chatbot for our support team.” That sentence names a possible interface, not the problem. Discovery must uncover who needs help, where work slows down, what decisions are made, which systems contain evidence and what a safe improvement would look like.

An FDE treats the initial request as a **hypothesis**. Building begins after the team can explain the current state with enough precision to test a smaller intervention.

## Map work, not presentation slides

<HandbookLearningVisual variant="fdeDiscovery" />

Follow one real case from trigger to outcome:

| Question | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|
| What starts the work? | Email, event, form, alert or human request |
| Who touches it? | Roles, handoffs, approvals and escalation paths |
| What decisions occur? | Rules, judgement calls and exceptions |
| Which systems matter? | Source of truth, read/write boundaries and identities |
| Where does it fail? | Rework, waiting, missing data and incorrect decisions |
| What proves improvement? | Time, quality, adoption, risk or revenue measure |

Interviews reveal beliefs. Observation reveals behaviour. Logs reveal system reality. Strong discovery triangulates all three.

## Simple analogy: diagnose before prescribing

A doctor does not prescribe treatment from “I feel tired” alone. They ask questions, observe symptoms and inspect evidence. An FDE treats the customer’s first feature request the same way: it is a starting symptom, not the final requirement.

## Real-time scenario: invoice exceptions

<Scenario title="The request is automation; the problem is missing evidence">

A finance leader asks for AI to approve invoices. Workflow observation shows that standard invoices already move quickly. Delays occur only when purchase orders, tax details or receiving records disagree. Staff spend time searching three systems and asking the requester for missing evidence.

The better first slice is not autonomous approval. It is an exception workspace that gathers the relevant records, explains the mismatch and drafts the follow-up. A human still decides. Discovery changed the solution from risky decision automation to useful evidence assembly.

</Scenario>

## Build a problem frame

Use this compact structure:

> When **[trigger]** happens, **[user]** needs to **[decision or action]**, but **[friction]** causes **[measurable impact]**. We will know the solution helps when **[metric]** improves without worsening **[guardrail]**.

Example: When an invoice enters exception status, an accounts-payable analyst needs to decide the next action, but evidence is split across three systems, adding two days of delay. We succeed if median resolution time falls by 40% without increasing incorrect payments or policy exceptions.

## Define success before architecture

Use a balanced scorecard:

- **Outcome:** Did the business process improve?
- **Task quality:** Was the output correct and useful?
- **Adoption:** Did intended users incorporate it into work?
- **Operations:** Did latency, reliability and cost meet limits?
- **Risk:** Were permissions, privacy and approvals preserved?

If success cannot be observed, the team cannot distinguish a useful deployment from a persuasive demo.

<PromptBox label="Convert a feature request into a problem frame">

You are helping with discovery, not selling a solution.

Initial request: [paste the stakeholder request]
Known users: [roles]
Known workflow: [current steps]

Produce:
1. The solution assumption hidden in the request
2. Ten questions grouped by workflow, data, decision, risk and adoption
3. A current-state workflow with unknowns clearly marked
4. A draft problem statement
5. One outcome metric and two guardrails
6. The smallest observation or experiment needed next

Do not propose architecture until the unknowns are visible.

</PromptBox>

## Discovery anti-patterns

<Mistakes>

- Interviewing only executives and not the people doing the work.
- Documenting the happy path while ignoring exceptions and escalations.
- Treating available data as trustworthy without checking ownership and quality.
- Choosing “number of AI responses” as the success metric.
- Promising automation before identifying actions that require human authority.

</Mistakes>

## Interview bit

<ThemeCard title="A customer asks for an AI agent. What do you do first?" badge="CAREER">

**Strong answer:** I would not start by selecting an agent framework. I would follow representative cases through the current workflow, identify users, decisions, systems, failure points and constraints, then define an outcome metric and risk guardrails. I would test whether retrieval, a deterministic workflow or a smaller assistance feature solves the bottleneck before adding agent autonomy.

</ThemeCard>

## Key takeaways

<Takeaways>

- The first customer request is usually a solution hypothesis.
- Observe real cases across happy paths and exceptions.
- Map decisions and system boundaries, not just screens.
- Define outcome, adoption, operational and risk measures together.
- Good discovery often makes the first build smaller.

</Takeaways>
