## The FDE paradox

An FDE creates value by getting close to one customer. The role creates leverage by ensuring the company does not solve the same problem from scratch for every customer.

The goal is not to eliminate all customer-specific work. Enterprise systems will always contain local data, policies and integrations. The goal is to separate **local configuration** from **repeated product capability**.

## Three layers of a deployment

| Layer | Examples | Long-term owner |
|---|---|---|
| Core product | Model gateway, permission system, evaluation service | Product engineering |
| Reusable deployment pattern | Connector template, workflow module, policy interface | Platform or solutions engineering |
| Customer configuration | Field mapping, thresholds, approved knowledge, roles | Customer operating team |

Confusion between these layers creates either a rigid product that cannot fit real work or an unmaintainable collection of customer forks.

## Simple analogy: create a reusable pattern, not one-size clothing

A tailor keeps a proven shirt pattern but adjusts measurements for each person. Productisation works the same way: preserve the repeated architecture and evaluation pattern while keeping customer data mappings and policies configurable.

## Real-time scenario: three claims deployments

<Scenario title="The connector is different; the pattern is the same">

Three insurers use different claims systems. Their data fields and approval policies vary, but every deployment needs document ingestion, evidence extraction, policy retrieval, uncertainty routing and an auditable decision packet.

The FDE team keeps mappings and thresholds configurable while productising the common workflow, evaluation hooks and audit model. The fourth deployment starts with a tested pattern instead of copied customer code.

</Scenario>

## Capture a pattern only when evidence repeats

After each deployment, record:

- The recurring user problem and outcome.
- Which constraints appear across customers.
- Which parts remained genuinely local.
- Repeated integrations, tools and evaluation needs.
- Operational failures and recovery patterns.
- Evidence that a reusable capability would reduce time or risk.

Do not productise the first customer’s preference as a universal requirement. Look for stable repetition across contexts.

## Design the handoff from day one

A strong handoff includes:

1. **Ownership:** named customer and product owners for operation and change.
2. **Runbook:** monitoring, incident, escalation and rollback procedures.
3. **Knowledge:** architecture decisions, configuration and known limits.
4. **Capability:** customer engineers can operate and modify the supported surface.
5. **Measures:** outcome, quality, adoption and risk dashboards remain visible.
6. **Feedback path:** recurring product gaps reach the responsible product team.

Handoff is not a final presentation. It is a gradual transfer proven through operation.

## Common mistakes

<Mistakes>

- Forking the product for every strategic customer.
- Generalising from one customer before a pattern repeats.
- Productising configuration while leaving evaluation and operations bespoke.
- Completing handoff without proving the customer can run the system.
- Measuring FDE success only through launch count instead of reusable leverage.

</Mistakes>

## Key takeaways

<Takeaways>

- Customer proximity should create product leverage, not permanent dependency.
- Separate core capability, reusable patterns and local configuration.
- Productise repeated evidence, not the loudest request.
- Handoff is an operational capability transfer.
- The mature FDE loop improves both customer outcomes and product deployability.

</Takeaways>
