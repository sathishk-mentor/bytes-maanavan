## What is generative AI?

<ThemeCard title="A clear definition" badge="Core concept" takeaway="Generative AI creates a new output by applying patterns learned during training to the instructions and context you provide.">

**Generative AI is a category of artificial intelligence that produces new content**—such as text, images, audio, video, software code or structured data—in response to an instruction.

A writing assistant can draft an email. An image model can create a visual from a description. A coding assistant can propose a function. The output did not exist in that exact form before the request, although it is created from patterns the model learned from training data.

This does **not** mean the system thinks or understands exactly like a person. It calculates a suitable output based on learned relationships, the current prompt, supplied context and its configuration.

</ThemeCard>

## What can generative AI create?

The word “content” is broader than writing. Depending on the model and application, generative AI can produce:

- **Text:** explanations, summaries, emails, plans and structured responses.
- **Images:** illustrations, product concepts and visual variations.
- **Audio and speech:** narration, transcription, sound and synthetic voices.
- **Video:** short clips, scene variations and animation.
- **Code and structured data:** functions, tests, SQL, JSON and format transformations.

The generated format does not prove that the result is accurate. A well-formatted table can still contain an incorrect value, just as a fluent paragraph can contain an invented fact.

## A simple real-life analogy

<Scenario title="The experienced chef analogy" context="Understanding generation without technical jargon">

Imagine an experienced chef who has studied thousands of recipes. You ask:

> “Create a quick vegetarian dinner using rice, tomato and paneer.”

The chef does not need to retrieve one exact recipe. Instead, the chef combines learned patterns—how ingredients work together, cooking techniques and your constraints—to propose a new dish.

Generative AI works in a comparable way. It has learned patterns from large collections of examples. Your prompt supplies the immediate goal and constraints. The model then generates a likely output.

## Traditional AI and generative AI

The difference becomes clearer when both systems receive a similar kind of input. Follow the two workflows below from left to right.

Traditional machine learning commonly returns a **label, score or prediction**. Generative AI returns **new content**. A real product may combine both—for example, a fraud model flags a transaction and a generative model drafts an investigation summary.

## How a generative AI request works

<ThemeCard title="Prompt-to-output flow" badge="Architecture">

![Architecture showing a user prompt passing through context preparation, a foundation model, safety checks and human review before becoming a usable result](/diagrams/genai-request-flow.svg)

1. **Instruction:** The user describes the required outcome.
2. **Context preparation:** The application adds relevant conversation, documents, examples or rules.
3. **Model inference:** A foundation model calculates and generates the response step by step.
4. **Safety and quality controls:** The application may filter unsafe material, validate formats or check sources.
5. **Human review:** A person verifies accuracy, suitability and business impact before important use.

The model is only one component. A dependable business application also needs good context, security controls, evaluation and responsible human oversight.

</ThemeCard>

## When should you use generative AI?

Use generative AI when producing or transforming a **draft** creates value and a person or system can verify the result. Increase the controls as the possible impact increases.

<GenAIDecisionGuide />

**A practical rule:** If a wrong answer could seriously affect health, safety, money, rights, employment or reputation, AI output must not become the final decision by itself.

## End-to-end workplace example

<ThemeCard title="Creating a weekly project update" badge="Case study">

Imagine that a project manager has meeting notes, completed work, open risks and next-week priorities. Instead of asking AI to “write a status report” without context, the manager follows a controlled workflow:

1. **Prepare:** Remove personal or confidential information that the approved tool should not receive.
2. **Provide context:** Supply the reporting period, approved notes, intended audience and required format.
3. **Generate:** Request a factual draft with separate sections for progress, risks, decisions and next actions.
4. **Verify:** Compare every name, date, percentage and commitment against the source notes.
5. **Own the decision:** The project manager edits and approves the update before sharing.

AI saves drafting time, but accountability remains with the project manager. The outcome is not merely “AI wrote the report”; it is “the manager produced a clearer, verified report with less repetitive work.”

</ThemeCard>

### Copy-paste prompt for the case study

<PromptBox label="Project update prompt">

You are assisting a project manager. Create a weekly project update using only the notes I provide.

Audience: Senior stakeholders
Tone: Clear, concise and factual
Required sections:
1. Progress this week
2. Decisions made
3. Risks or blockers
4. Next-week priorities
5. Actions with owner and due date

Rules:
- Do not invent missing names, dates or percentages.
- Mark missing information as [NEEDS CONFIRMATION].
- Keep the update under 350 words.
- End with a verification checklist.

Project notes:
[PASTE APPROVED NOTES HERE]

</PromptBox>

## A practical mini exercise

**Try this prompt in a generative AI assistant. Do not include confidential information:**

<PromptBox label="Learning explanation prompt">

You are helping a beginner understand cloud computing.

Explain cloud computing in three formats:
1. A definition in no more than 40 words.
2. A real-life analogy using a rented apartment.
3. A two-sentence interview answer.

Keep technical terms in English. Use simple language and identify one limitation of the analogy.

</PromptBox>

After receiving the answer, check:

- Did each format serve a different purpose?
- Was the analogy helpful without being technically misleading?
- Did the response invent facts?
- What additional context would improve it?

## From weak prompt to useful prompt

<ThemeCard title="Why prompt quality changes the result" badge="Prompt design">

**Weak prompt:** “Explain cloud.”

The task, learner level, length and expected format are unclear. The model must guess what “good” means.

The improved exercise specifies the audience, three output formats, word limit, analogy and quality check. These constraints make the response easier to evaluate.

A useful beginner pattern is: **Role + task + context + constraints + output format + verification rule.** This cannot guarantee truth, but it makes the request and result easier to inspect.

</ThemeCard>

## What generative AI cannot guarantee

<Mistakes>

- **“A confident answer must be correct.”** Language models can produce fluent but inaccurate information. Verify important claims using reliable sources.
- **“The model is a database.”** A model generates responses from learned patterns. Connect it to trusted data and retrieval when current or organisation-specific facts are required.
- **“More text means a better prompt.”** Useful context, a clear task, constraints and an output format matter more than unnecessary length.
- **“AI output is ready to publish.”** Review privacy, accuracy, copyright, bias, tone and potential impact before external or high-stakes use.

</Mistakes>

Generative AI is useful for drafting, exploring, transforming and accelerating work. It should not be treated as an unquestionable authority. NIST’s generative AI risk guidance emphasises managing trustworthiness risks throughout the AI lifecycle, not only after deployment.

## Interview bit

<ThemeCard title="How would you explain generative AI in an interview?" badge="Career">

**Short answer:**

Generative AI is a type of AI that creates new content such as text, images, audio or code from a prompt. It uses patterns learned during model training to generate an output that fits the instruction and supplied context.

**Strong follow-up:**

Unlike a traditional classifier that may return a label or probability, a generative model produces content. In a production application, I would combine the model with approved context, security controls, evaluation, output validation and human review based on the risk of the task.

**Possible follow-up questions:**

- What is a foundation model?
- Why can a generative model hallucinate?
- How is generative AI different from an AI agent?
- When would you use retrieval-augmented generation?

</ThemeCard>

## Key takeaways

<Takeaways>

- Generative AI produces new content in response to instructions and context.
- It learns patterns during training; it does not simply search for one stored answer.
- Traditional AI often predicts or classifies, while generative AI creates content.
- Useful applications combine a model with context, controls, evaluation and human judgement.
- Fluent output can still be wrong, so important results must be verified.

</Takeaways>
